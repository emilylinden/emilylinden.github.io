Below is all of my advice for applications. I tried to include enough detail so that if you have no idea where to start in the process, this page will walk you through it. 

*Because application conventions and expectations differ between grad programs, my advice is most applicable to people applying to PhD programs in Physics at universities in the US. However, my impression is that the advice translates perfectly fine to any science or engineering (US) PhD program. I begin with a checklist of everything you have to do to get the applications done, and then I go into detailed advice for each item.*
<!-- *Context in which to interpret my advice: I applied for physics programs to study theoretical quantum science. I applied to 10 US universities for the Fall of 2024 and was accepted to 4.*-->

## Checklist (Semi-chronological):
1. Ask 3-4 professors to be your letter of recommendation writers.
2. Make a list of grad schools you want to apply to, and check **fee waivers**.
3. (Optional) Reach out to professors at your prospective schools.
4. Write your Statement of Purpose (SOP)
5. Write other application essays and gather other materials (transcript, CV)
6. Submit!
7. (Optional) Apply to fellowships/scholarships.

## 1. Letters of Rec
If you've already applied to undergrad, you're probably familiar with the concept of a letter of recommendation (LOR). A LOR is written by a professor who can speak to your research experience, academic prowess, or even teaching/outreach/volunteering contributions. It is most important that this person can attest to your intellectual merits, not your personal ones (as might have been the case in undergrad). To apply to a PhD program, a given school will require you to submit 3 LOR, but sometimes asking a 4th person to write you a LOR is a good security measure. The nice thing about LORs is that they don't take much work from you besides emailing the professors; once you politely ask a professor to write you a LOR, they will know what to do. You don't need to feel apologetic or burdensome about asking, as this is part of their job and they are expecting to write these. However, you should do everything in your power to make their job as easy as possible.

### Everything you need to send to a recommender:
* Initial request: you really want to ask professors to be your recommenders at least a **4-6 weeks** before the application deadline, but the earlier the better. Below is an email template to ask someone to be your recommender.
* List of schools: whenever you finalize the list of schools that you want to apply to, you need to send it to your recommenders in an organized table with corresponding submission deadline dates **and times** (pay attention to time zones). However, if you haven't finalized your list yet, don't let that delay you from sending the initial request email.
* Application materials: you should also send your recommenders any application materials you have, like your SOP or CV, which will help them write their letter and make sure their narrative lines up with yours.
* Reminders: Just because they agree to write you a letter doesn't mean they will remember about it a month or two later—it's up to you to make sure they get it in. Tip: sending your application materials (list of schools, SOP, CV) can serve as a reminder that is not awkward or pushy! It's your discretion how many reminders you send, but I would recommend sending one ~3 weeks out from the deadline, and one within a week of the deadline. You can monitor when they submit their LOR through the application portals, and send one more reminder day-of or day before if they still haven't submitted.

### Letter request email template:
*Subject: Letter of Rec*

*Hello Professor ________,*

*I hope this email finds you well. I am planning to apply to [Physics] PhD programs for the Fall 202[7] semester, and I was wondering if you would be willing to write me a letter of recommendation in support of my applications. The earliest deadlines are [in the beginning of December]. Please let me know if you would like to meet with me to discuss in more detail, or if you need anything else from me at this time. If you are able to be my recommender, then I will send you my finalized list of schools (and deadlines) as well as my other application materials when I have them.*

*Looking forward to hearing from you.*


*Sincerely,*

## 2. List of Schools
Figuring out your list of schools that you want to apply to is a nontrivial step, and I for one had no idea where to start. You want to aim to have a list of anywhere from 8 to 12 schools (some people go up to 20). I applied to 10. 

### Where to start?
Ask a mentor (professor or grad student working in your area of interest) to recommend a few schools that might be hubs for your area of interest. You can also research online. Another thing that can help guide you is which schools offer fee waivers. Grad school applications can be expensive ($50-$150 each), so it's worth looking into. Check out the Big 10 Alliance, where you can [apply](https://btaa.org/resources-for/students/freeapp/eligibility) to get the application fees waived for participating schools. Several schools also have internal fee waivers that you can apply for in the main application portal itself. If all else fails, start looking into schools that are in an area you'd like to live for the next several years.

### Criteria:
Many people have different criteria for narrowing down their list, but I went off of a singular metric: amount of professors whose research interests me and with whom I would like to work (ie. **number of potential advisors**). Here was my method: for a given school, go to the department website of the program you’re applying to (ex. Physics), go to the faculty page, then go through literally every professor and, if you are interested in their research, add them to a list of potential advisors for that school. If in the end you find at least 2 but preferably 3-4 potential advisors you’d be interested in working with, then you probably want to apply to that school. The method was effective in narrowing down my list, but if you have broader research interests then maybe you can afford applying other filters to narrow it down, like location.

## 3. Reaching out to potential advisors
This is one of my biggest pieces of advice: either while you're working on applications or right after you submit them, reach out to the professors you’re interested in working with and ask to meet with them. Take this opportunity to ask them about their research and any other questions you have about the school/program/their group so they know who you are when it’s time to review applications (and so that you have more data for when it comes time to make your own decision!). There’s no harm in doing this. They might not respond or have time to meet with you, but that’s okay. I think a good time to do this would be around November. Below is a template for how to write this email.

### Reaching out to prospective advisors email template:
*Subject: Prospective PhD Student Inquiry*

*Dear Dr. ______,*

*My name is ________ and I am a senior physics major at [Princeton University]. I will be graduating in Spring 202[7], and I will be applying to the Physics PhD program at [CU Boulder] for the Fall 202[7] semester.*

*I am reaching out because I am interested in your research on ________. [...talk about your research experience...]*

*I am interested in studying ___________ in graduate school. Will there be any openings in your lab for a graduate student starting in Fall 202[7]? The ________ Lab aligns with my interests and I would love to set up a time to discuss joining your lab if you think it could be a good fit.*

*I've attached my CV and would be happy to answer any questions you may have about my experience. I look forward to hearing from you.*


*Sincerely,*

## 4. Statement of Purpose (SOP)
### What is it? 
An SOP is a 1-2 page written document that answers the question, “Why are you applying to this program?” There is a lot of stuff packed in that “why”: why do you want to go to grad school? Why do you want to study X? (What do you want to study?) Why do you want to go to *this* school and *this* program? And, equally important, why should they choose you?

It's not just a place to throw everything you want them to know about you. It should be a focused essay with a thesis statement and a consistent narrative. It should not have any personal mushy stuff in it (you can save that for the personal essays). It should be about science. You can and should still show passion and authenticity, but it should be technical and professional. The audience for your SOP is professors that work in the field you're applying for. Your end goal is to convince them your experiences have given you clarity, confidence, commitment, and enthusiasm in you desire to go to graduate school.

### Lots of advice here:
* Somewhere near the beginning, you need to have a ‘thesis statement’ that says exactly what you want to study/work on in grad school. It should be very clear and explicit.
* At some point (I did it at the end) you’re going to include the **specific names** of professors in the department you’re applying to that you’d like to work with. If you have room, you can read up on some papers from each one and give a couple words about what each one works on when you mention them in the SOP (eg. "I am interested by Professor Smith's work on X") so you seem very informed.
* Most of the SOP will be taken up by you talking about research experience (if any), classes, awards, clubs and any other things that make you qualified. Writing about your research experience is the most important part. Be technical, show that you understand what you were working on, what question you were trying to solve (both the overarching question of the research you're contributing to and the specific smaller scale question of your project), highlight your contributions, what worked and what didn't work, what you learned, whether it changed your interests. Tell a story. Note: it doesn't matter if your contributions seem unimportant; what matters much more is that you can explain your contributions, explain the science, explain the question you were trying to answer, credit other people with what they did, etc. 
*  Don’t forget to make it clear that you enjoy the stuff you work on/study; that can easily get lost in writing but it is very important to make it come through.
*  Don't be afraid to brag—it might be uncomfortable but unfortunately you have to do it.
* Don’t brush off the question, “Why do you want to go to grad school?” Let them know that you understand the unique challenge of grad school and that you’re committed to it for xyz reasons.
* Also don’t forget to answer the question “Why this school and program?”. Can just be 1 maybe 2 sentences, but make them think that they're your #1 choice of school by putting specifics about the placement of the school, the connections the school has to other institutions, the diversity of the school, the format of the program, the initiatives the department has, etc. 
* If you have any experience with teaching/tutoring, public outreach, volunteering, supporting underrepresented groups, etc, let them know. If you’re highlighting it more in a different essay you can keep it brief. It’s very good in academia to be interested in this stuff.
* All grad schools require the SOP and are expecting this same thing, so you can keep it very similar between schools and just tailor between schools.

## 5. Other application materials
Your other main application materials are your CV, personal essays, and transcript. I don't have much advice here, but:
* For your CV, pretend that someone with the attention span of a second grader is reading it. Make it as easy as possible to glean the important/impressive things, especially if they don't appear elsewhere in your application.
* For your personal essays: the prompts vary widely between schools and it's unclear how much they care about these, but it can still serve some good purposes: (a) if there is anything that needs explaining (eg. low GPA, gap years), you can do it here and offer some explanation for it (some schools have an optional essay just for this purpose); (b) if you care about teaching, outreach, volunteering, improving education access, etc., you can expand more here (I think they can be suckers for this because people like this are very good for the culture in academia); (c) if you have a story that demonstrates resilience, that is an important quality for a PhD student and you can share that here; (d) you can make yourself human by talking about something you're actually interested in. People like authenticity.
* For your transcript: I have no idea how much GPA matters, in case you were going to ask. 
* Other: Most applications have a section where they ask you to list all the other schools you're applying to—just leave this blank.

## 6. Submission
I offer the following obvious advice: you should actually open the application portal and fill out the boring stuff **before** the day it is due, because this takes longer than you expect. Application portals are not standardized between schools and they all have a bunch of random questions or requests (like inputting all of your upper level coursework with corresponding textbooks into their system), so you shouldn't wait for surprises day-of. I also encourage you to tailor your SOP in advance, because it might take longer than you think to get it to the specific word count, or chop up the material in a different way according to the essays each school expects. 

## 7. Fellowships
If you have time, apply to fellowships like NSF and Quad, or any internal scholarships the schools have. If you do apply for these, you can include this info in your application which advisors like to see. Unfortunately, most of the fellowships I'm aware of are reserved for US citizens.
